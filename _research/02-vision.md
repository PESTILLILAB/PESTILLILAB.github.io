Visual cortex holds several maps of the visual field. In more than 1,700 brains, from age 2 to 88, we found that the wiring between visual areas respects those maps: regions that represent the same location are preferentially connected, and the connections share the asymmetries of perception. We see better along the horizontal meridian than the vertical one, and in the lower visual field than the upper one, and the connections follow the same pattern. We are now testing the axonal basis of these rules in macaque tracing data and human post-mortem tissue, and asking whether measured wiring improves predictions of perception. This work grows out of a long-standing interest in how attention changes what we see.
